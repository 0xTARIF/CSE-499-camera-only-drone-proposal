Look at this picture from a drone's camera. This picture is my own illustration. Can you spot the wire? It is there, but it is very thin. This is the problem I want to talk about: can a drone with one cheap camera learn to fly safely around thin obstacles?

Drones that fly with just one camera are cheap, about $290, compared with about $5,489 for a drone that carries lasers and extra sensors. Both prices are approximate and come from the SVE paper. That paper, by Wu and colleagues, was published in Expert Systems with Applications, and it already shows that one camera is enough to fly through big red gates.

But the real world also has thin things like wires, bamboo poles and branches. In the drone's picture, a wire may be only one to three tiny dots wide. That figure comes from the TTPLA dataset paper, which studied power lines in large 4K aerial images, so in a smaller drone image a wire can be even thinner. It is like trying to spot a single hair on a carpet from across the room. It also blends into the trees and buildings behind it, and missing it means a crash.

Other researchers score themselves with a number called IoU, which stands for intersection over union. It simply tells you how well the computer's answer matches the correct answer: 1 is a perfect match, 0 is no match at all. EDFNet scored 0.007, which is less than 1 percent overlap, so it almost never found the thinnest wires. UCorr scored 0.138, about 14 percent, so it still missed most of the wire. Both scores come from computer-made pictures, like a video game, and both only detect the wire. Two more examples: Wei and colleagues built an orchard drone with one camera, and it needed human help near protruding branches. And VLM-Nav, published in PLOS ONE this year, left trees, poles and lamps out of its tests, and it was tested only in simulation.

So most of this work only finds the thin object, like spotting a pothole from the passenger seat without ever steering the car around it. As far as my search shows, very few systems fly a camera-only drone around thin obstacles. That is the gap, and that is what my project goes after.

2  ·  How SVE works

One picture in, one flight command out

How does the drone turn a camera picture into a flight command?
Figure 2 from Wu et al.[1], reproduced under CC BY 4.0. Red boxes show the part being explained.
Part 0 of 7
The big picture
  1. The camera takes a picture.
  2. We shake the picture a little with random noise.
  3. A transformer (ViT) looks at it and picks a flight command.
  4. The drone moves. The camera takes a new picture. Repeat.
No depth sensor. No map. One network: picture in, command out.
Part 1 of 7
Input and output
  1. In: a colour picture. Every pixel has 3 numbers: red, green, blue.
  2. Out: 4 speed numbers: forward, sideways, up or down, and turn.
Equation 1v = πSVE( IRGB )
DoesSays the whole drone brain is one function: picture in, command out.
HowThe network looks at the picture only. Nothing else.
ExampleA gate is on the left of the picture. The command can be: go forward, move a little left, turn left (made-up numbers).
Part 2 of 7
Stable Variation: the key idea
  1. The picture is the centre (μ). We do not change it.
  2. σ is a table of “shake sizes”, one for each pixel. It is the same for every picture.
  3. ξ is random noise: one random number for each pixel.
  4. Picture + σ × noise = the sample.
Equations 4, 5, 9Isample = IRGB + σ ⊙ ξ
DoesMakes a slightly shaken copy of the picture.
How⊙ means multiply pixel by pixel. Big σ: the pixel shakes a lot. Small σ: it hardly moves.
ExamplePixel brightness 200, σ = 5, noise ξ = −0.4. Sample = 200 + 5 × (−0.4) = 198. A pixel with σ = 0 stays 200. (made-up numbers)
Part 3 of 7
The ViT decoder
  1. Input Embedding: cut the picture into small 4×4 squares. Each square becomes 1024 numbers.
  2. Positional Encoding: give each square a position tag.
  3. Repeat 6 times: Norm, Attention (squares look at each other), Norm, Feed Forward. Skip arrows keep old information.
  4. Output: the 4 speed numbers.
Equation 3v = ViT[ SV[ IRGB ] ]
DoesSays: first shake the picture (SV), then read it (ViT).
HowSV only adds noise. It has no network. All the thinking is inside the ViT.
ExampleThe squares near the gate look at each other and agree: “gate on the left, turn left.”
Part 4 of 7
Learning from an expert
  1. In the AirSim simulator an expert knows where the gates are. It gives the right command.
  2. The model looks at the same picture and guesses.
  3. The gap between guess and expert is the loss. We change the ViT and σ to make it smaller.
Equation 2Θ* = argmin E[ Dist( π*(E), πSVEΘ(IRGB) ) ]
DoesFinds the settings with the smallest average gap to the expert.
ExampleExpert: turn left 10°. Model: turn left 4°. Gap = 6, squared = 36. Training makes this number small.
Equation 11∇σL = Eξ[ ∇IsampleL ⊙ ξ ]
DoesTells us how to change σ, even though the noise is random.
ExampleIf more shake at a pixel makes mistakes bigger, σ goes down there. (We average over many noise draws.)
Part 5 of 7
Why it is called stable
  1. Normal VAE: an encoder looks at the picture and decides the noise size. A tiny picture change (δ) changes the noise size.
  2. SVE: σ never looks at the picture. The noise size stays the same.
Normal VAE, Eq. 8
σ²(I+δ) ≈ σ²(I) + Jδ
Light flickers a little: noise size jumps 5 → 9.
SVE, Eq. 10
∂Isample / ∂I = identity
Light flickers a little: noise size stays 5.
DoesA small camera change stays a small change inside the model (Lipschitz constant 1).
HowJ = how strongly the noise size reacts to the picture. Real cameras always have tiny changes from light and shaking. Numbers 5 and 9 are made up.
Think ofA radio. A normal VAE changes the volume every time the signal wiggles. SVE keeps the volume fixed.

Closing the loop
  1. The UAV controller turns the command into motor action.
  2. The drone moves in the environment.
  3. The monocular camera takes a new picture.
  4. This repeats about 12.7 times every second.
No long-term plan. The drone reacts to every new picture, like a driver who watches the road all the time.

[Part 0, about 40 seconds] This slide answers one question: how does the drone turn a camera picture into a flight command? Look at Figure 2 from the paper. It is a loop, and I will walk around it once. A camera picture goes in on the left. A small random shake is added to the picture. A transformer reads it and gives a flight command. The drone moves, the camera takes a new picture, and we start again. The red dashed line shows this loop. The important point is what is missing: there is no depth sensor, no laser, and no map. It is one network, picture in, command out. Think of a person who looks at the road and moves the steering wheel, with no GPS. In the next parts I will explain each box, and every equation, with a small example.

[Part 1] First box: the input and the output. The input is a normal colour picture. Each pixel has three numbers, for red, green and blue. The output is four numbers: how fast to go forward, sideways, and up or down, and how much to turn. In the figure these are the little coloured stack called v, with vx, vy, vz and v-psi. Equation 1 just says that the whole system is one function. You put in a picture, you get out a command. Example: if a red gate is on the left of the picture, the command could say go forward, move a little left and turn left. These numbers are made up, only to show the idea. If someone asks, 'where is the depth sensor?', the answer is: there is none. The model learns depth from the picture alone.

[Part 2, the key idea of the paper, take your time] Here is what the authors call Stable Variation. Step one: the picture is the centre. In the figure it says mu equals image. We do not change it. Step two: the Deviation Embedding, called sigma. Imagine a sheet as big as the picture, and each pixel has a number that says how much that pixel is allowed to shake. The sheet is learned during training, and it is the same for every picture. It never looks at the picture. Step three: random noise, called xi, one random number for every pixel. Step four: we add sigma times noise to the picture, and call the result the sample. The circle-dot sign in the equation means multiply pixel by pixel. Example with made-up numbers: a pixel has brightness 200, sigma is 5, and the noise number is minus 0.4. Then 5 times minus 0.4 is minus 2, so the sample pixel is 198. A pixel with sigma zero stays at 200. So the picture is shaken slightly, and the shake size is decided by sigma. Be honest here: after training, sigma is fixed, but the noise xi is still random every time. Why add shake at all? The authors say a little randomness helps the model work well on pictures it has not seen, without losing accuracy.

[Part 3] Now the decoder, a Vision Transformer, which is the 'thinking' part. First, Input Embedding: the sample picture is cut into small squares of 4 by 4 pixels, called patches. Each patch is turned into a list of 1024 numbers. Second, Positional Encoding: each patch gets a tag, so the model knows where it was. Without the tag, it could not tell left from right. Third, the block in the figure repeats six times. Norm keeps the numbers in a safe range. Attention, with 8 heads, lets every patch look at every other patch and decide which are important. Feed Forward mixes the information more. The skip arrows add the old information back, so nothing gets lost. Last, we get the four speed numbers. Equation 3 is only a short way to write it: first stable variation, then the ViT. Stable variation has no network inside, it only adds noise. All learning power is in the ViT. The paper calls it decoder-only, because a normal VAE also has an encoder, and SVE does not. Example: patches around the gate look at each other and the model decides, gate on the left, so turn left.

[Part 4] How does the model learn? By copying an expert. This is called imitation learning. In the AirSim simulator, the expert knows exactly where the gates are, so it always knows the right command. The model sees the same picture and makes a guess. The gap between the guess and the expert is called the loss. Equation 2 says: find the settings that make this gap smallest on average. Example: the expert says turn left 10 degrees, the model says 4 degrees. The gap is 6, and squared it is 36. After more training, this number gets small. Equation 11 is about sigma. The noise is random, so we cannot take a normal slope directly. The trick is to multiply the slope at the noisy picture by the noise, and average it over many draws. That lets sigma learn in the same way as the other weights. Simple example: if more shake at one pixel makes the mistakes bigger, sigma goes down at that pixel. Training details from the paper: about 2 million pictures, 200 epochs, Adam optimizer with learning rate 1e-4, and batch size 64.

[Part 5, why the name is stable] Here we compare with a normal VAE. In a normal VAE, an encoder looks at the picture and decides how big the noise should be. So if the picture changes a tiny bit, for example because the light flickers or the drone shakes, the noise size also changes. Equation 8 says this: the new noise size is the old one plus J times the small change delta. J is called the Jacobian, and it just means how strongly the noise size reacts to the picture. A big J means a jumpy input for the decoder. Example with made-up numbers: noise size jumps from 5 to 9 after a small flicker. In SVE, sigma does not look at the picture, so the noise size stays 5. Equation 10 says a small change in the picture stays a small change inside the model. The paper calls this a Lipschitz constant of 1. Think of a radio: a normal VAE changes the volume every time the signal wiggles, SVE keeps the volume fixed. In the paper, the VAE version got 0 percent success in simulation. Honest note: this is the authors' own argument. It is partly simple, because any noise that does not depend on the picture behaves like this.

[Part 6] Finally we close the loop in the figure. The command goes to the UAV controller, which turns it into motor actions. The drone moves in the environment. The monocular camera, which means a one-eye camera, takes a new picture, and the cycle starts again. It runs about 12.7 times every second. There is no long-term plan. The drone looks at every new picture and reacts, like a driver who keeps watching the road. This is also why thin obstacles are a problem: if the wire is almost invisible in this one picture, there is nothing else to save the drone.

[Part 7, then move to slide 3] The paper's results: on a real drone, SVE passed in 75 percent of 20 trials, a plain ViT in about 40 percent, and the VAE got 0 percent in simulation. Please keep four honest points in mind. One, sigma is fixed after training, but the noise is still random. Two, the stability argument is partly simple. Three, 20 real flights is a small test. Four, and most important for us: the tests use big red gates, not thin wires. So we do not know yet if SVE works for thin obstacles. That is a question, not a result, and it is where our project starts. Next slide: where SVE may fail, and what other papers do not cover.

Everyday example: finding a red ball on a table is easy; finding a single hair on a carpet is hard. A one-camera drone has the same problem. Important: I am not saying SVE fails. I am saying nobody has shown that it works.

[Part 1] What did the SVE paper actually test? Big red gates, in the AirSim simulator and with a real drone. On the real drone, SVE succeeded in 75 percent of 20 trials, and a plain ViT in about 40 percent. That is a good result. But look at the list: wires, poles, branches and bamboo are not in the tests, as far as I read the paper. So the result is good, but for a different problem. Example: a driver who only practised on wide roads has not shown that they can drive on a narrow lane. If someone asks, 'maybe it already works on wires?', the honest answer is: maybe, but there is no evidence, and that is exactly why we test it.

[Part 2, interactive, take your time] Here is the first reason SVE might struggle. Remember from slide 2: SVE adds noise equal to sigma times a random number, and sigma is one fixed table, the same for every picture. The noise cannot see the picture. That is good for stability, but a wire can be anywhere, so sigma cannot know where the wire is. It shakes the wire pixels as hard as the sky. On the left is a clean picture with a thick bar, like a gate post, and a thin wire. Both are exactly 50 brightness levels darker than the sky. On the right is the picture after noise. The real sigma is learned and may be small on purpose, so we must measure it.

[Part 3, interactive] Second reason: picture size. A drone has to be fast. SVE runs at about 12.7 pictures per second. Fast usually means small pictures, because big pictures take more computing time, and a ViT cuts the picture into many patches. On the left is the big picture, with a 2-pixel wire. On the right is the same picture shrunk and then enlarged, so you can see the blocky pixels the computer sees. The wire gets thinner than one pixel and turns grey: its contrast falls. The thick bar is still clear. In numbers: at half size the wire is 1 pixel wide, at a quarter size it is half a pixel wide and only about half as dark. Again, these numbers are an illustration. We must measure the real picture size used by SVE and by our own experiments.

[Part 4, interactive] Third reason: training data. SVE learns by copying an expert. In the paper the expert flew through gates, with about 2 million pictures. Look at the moving film strip: every picture has a gate. The counter on the right says wires: zero, as far as I read the paper. That means the expert never had to avoid a wire, so the model never learned that move. Example: a student who only practised on big gates meets a wire for the first time in the exam. This is our plan: we will build wire pictures in the simulator, with an expert that knows where the wires are, so the model has examples to copy. This also connects to a hard part we already know: we need expert demonstrations for wires, and we will have to build them.

[Part 5, interactive] So how do we check these three ideas? We do not guess; we test. Step one: in the AirSim simulator we build wires of different widths: 1, 2, 3 and 5 pixels. Step two: we fly a plain ViT and a plain SVE on every width. Step three: we plot success rate against wire width. The empty chart shows our plan. A: SVE stays strong. Then SVE is already a good base, and our idea needs to show a smaller extra gain. B: SVE drops on thin wires. Then our hypothesis holds, and thin wires are a real weak spot where we can help. C: SVE is no better than ViT. Then we need something new. These curves are only possible shapes, not data. Whatever we find, we report it. That is honest research, and it is also a result we can publish.

[Part 6, interactive] Now, what do other papers cover? First, thin-obstacle detectors, like EDFNet and UCorr, and the TTPLA dataset. Second, depth-based avoidance, like seVAE with CPN, and ViT with LSTM. They do fly, but they need a depth sensor, which adds cost and weight. Third, camera-only flight: SVE, the orchard drone, VLM-Nav. They use one camera and they fly, but thin obstacles are not tested: the orchard drone needed human help at branches, and VLM-Nav left out poles and lamps. Fourth, noise in imitation learning, like DART and Bayesian Disturbance Injection. As I read them, they add noise to actions or demonstrations, not to the picture with a learned map. I have not read every detail, so I will re-check them before the talk. The last row, in gold, is our target: all three ticks.

[Part 7] Here is the gap, in one sentence: camera-only, end-to-end flight around thin obstacles, with noise that knows where the lines are. [Part 0, about 40 seconds] This is our idea. I want to be clear: it is a proposal. We have not tested it yet. Remember slide 2: SVE adds random noise to the picture, and the size of the noise comes from one fixed table, the same for every pixel. Slide 3 showed that the same noise on a thin wire could bury it. Our idea is to let the noise size depend on where the lines are. Look at the two pictures. On the left is SVE: the same orange everywhere, which means the same noise size everywhere. On the right is ours: the noise size is bigger or smaller along the wire. Everything else stays the same: the same stable idea, the same ViT, only the noise map changes. Think of dust on a window. SVE puts the same dust everywhere. We put dust on purpose, along the lines. The direction of the change, bigger or smaller, is a question that the experiment will answer.

[Part 1, interactive] Step one is to find the lines. We use a few fixed line filters. They slide over the picture, and each one looks for a thin line at one angle. We use 8 angles. They are fixed, so there is nothing to train. The result is a line map called M, with a number for each pixel: zero means no line, one means a clear line. How does a filter work? For each pixel it compares the pixel with its two sides across the line. A thin dark wire is darker than both sides. A thick bar is not, because one of its sides is also dark. Look at the demo: on the right is the line map. The wire lights up, the thick bar and the sky stay white. The three numbers below show the average line map on the wire, on the bar and on the sky. Past about three pixels the wire is no longer thin, and the line map fades. That is exactly the point: the filter is built for thin structures. This is a real calculation, but on a made-up picture.

[Part 2, interactive, the heart of the slide] Step two: build the noise map. The formula is sigma of I equals sigma-base times one plus alpha times M. Sigma-base is the learned table from SVE. M is the line map. Alpha is one knob. Where M is zero, the noise is the same as SVE. Where M is one, the noise is one plus alpha times bigger. Example with made-up numbers: sigma-base is 10 and alpha is 2. On the sky the noise size is 10. On the wire it is 10 times three, which is 30. If alpha is minus 0.7, the wire gets 10 times 0.3, which is 3: almost no noise. The first picture is the scene. The second is the noise map: dark red means big noise. The third is the noisy result. Alpha equals zero is plain SVE. Alpha equals plus 2 is more shake on lines: this is the original idea of emphasising lines. The wire gets harder to see, and the model has to learn to find it through the noise. It might become more robust, or it might fail. Alpha equals minus 0.7 protects the lines: the wire stays clear. Look at the box at the bottom: contrast divided by noise on the wire. Above 2 is easy. So which direction is better? We do not know. Both are in the test.

[Part 3] Step three is the same as before. We add the noise with our new map, and the same ViT reads it. In the picture, the gold boxes are new: the line filters, the line map, and the sigma map. The navy boxes are the same as SVE. The equation is the same as on slide 2: I-sample equals I plus sigma times xi. The only difference is that sigma is now a map that depends on the picture, and no longer a fixed table. Xi is random noise as before. The line filters are small next to the ViT, so we expect little extra cost, but we will measure the speed, because the real drone runs at about 12.7 pictures per second and we do not want to slow it down.

[Part 4, interactive, the key design point] This is the most important question about our idea. In SVE, sigma does not depend on the picture. That is why it is stable. In a normal VAE, it does, and the paper says this makes it unstable. Our sigma depends on the picture again, so we must be careful. Our answer: the picture only enters through the fixed line filters, and the line map is clipped between zero and one. So the noise size can change by at most sigma-base times the size of alpha. Example: sigma-base is 5 and alpha is 2. Then sigma can never move by more than 10, whatever happens to the picture. A normal VAE has no such limit. Another helpful fact: a brightness flicker adds the same amount to every pixel, and the line filter looks at differences, so it cancels. The first two pictures show the clean and the flickering picture. The third shows how much the line map changed: almost nothing. The bars show how much sigma jumps. Our bar is a real computation and stays small, below the gold limit. The VAE bar uses a made-up scale, so please say 'illustration'. Be honest: we still have to prove this bound and measure it on the drone. And remember, SVE's own stability argument is partly simple too, so we will not oversell ours.

[Part 5, interactive] To teach the model about wires, we need data. In the AirSim simulator we draw wires of width 1, 2, 3 and 5 pixels. An expert that knows where each wire is gives the right command for each picture. This is called label-consistent: the command in the label must match what the wire really means for the drone. If the label does not match the picture, the model learns the wrong link. We train on gates and wires together. These commands are made-up examples. Later we add an indoor test with a thin fishing line, and real pictures of wires and bamboo. That part comes on slide 5. This also solves one of the hard parts we listed at the start: where do the expert demonstrations come from? Answer: we build them in the simulator.

[Part 6, interactive] Now, how will we know if it works? We compare six methods, using the same data and the same ViT. One: a plain ViT, with no noise. Two: plain SVE, which is our alpha equals zero. Three: SVE plus an edge channel. This is the simple competitor: we just give the ViT an extra edge picture. If that already works, our idea must beat it, or we say so. Four: ours with alpha larger than zero, more shake on lines. Five: ours with alpha smaller than zero, less shake on lines. Six, optional: the closest related noise methods, like the noise-based learning paper and DART. They show how much comes from our idea and how much from just adding noise. The measures are: success rate against wire width, number of crashes, speed in pictures per second, and how much sigma jumps under flicker. And we promise to report the result even if our method is not the best.

If the tests agree, we can claim four things: a line-aware noise map that stays bounded, a wire-width test for camera-only flight, a wire dataset with expert labels, and an analysis of when stable variation breaks. We do not claim that noise is new. Older noise methods exist, like noise-based learning and DART. We do not claim that it works yet. And we do not claim it beats everything. To my knowledge, based on my literature search, I did not find this exact combination. Risks: it may help less than we hope.
